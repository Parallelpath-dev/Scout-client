One-time analysis

Collected 20 August 2026, covering the 13 weeks from 20 May. These numbers do not refresh. The rest of Scout updates every Monday; this page is a point-in-time study of 234 posts across 9 accounts, and it stays as it is unless we run it again.

The headline finding

Syndicated content is the weakest content on every account that receives it

Before you believe it

How hard we tried to break this

A pattern this convenient deserves suspicion. The effect depends entirely on how tightly "the same drop" is defined — and that is the point. Loosen the window and the signal dissolves into ordinary posting overlap, which is exactly what should happen if the tight clusters are a real, distinct behaviour rather than an artifact of coincidence.

Read this as a guardrail, not a weakness. If the effect had persisted at a six-hour window it would mean we were measuring "posting at similar times," not coordinated syndication. It does not persist, so we are not.

What we can prove

Three to five market accounts published within the same 15-minute window, in the same format, eight separate times in thirteen weeks. Those posts underperform their own market's baseline by a wide and consistent margin.

What we are inferring

That the creative is centrally produced and distributed. We can see the timestamps, not the asset. Ask the question in the room rather than asserting it — if the answer is "no, coincidence," that is still worth knowing.

The evidence

Eight coordinated drops

Each row is one moment when three or more markets posted simultaneously. The score is the median robust z across that drop — how far it landed from each receiving market's own normal for that format. Zero means "performed exactly like a typical post on that account."

The network

Market scorecard

Every market judged against itself, then compared on rates rather than totals. Reel efficiency is engagement per thousand plays — a true rate against actual distribution, available because Instagram exposes play counts publicly. Reach index is plays per thousand followers: how far the algorithm carries a market's video relative to its audience size.

The lever

Reach is not the same as resonance

Two different questions, deliberately kept on two different charts. How far does a market's video travel, and how hard does it work once it gets there? The markets that win one are not the markets that win the other.

Where a human takes over

The review queue

The system produces flags, not verdicts. Every post below sits outside its own market's normal range for its format — but the machine cannot see what the video was about, whether the gym was closed that week, or whether a post did an operational job that engagement will never measure. A strategist reads these, tags the content theme, and decides what reaches the market teams. Links go to the live posts.

The ceiling

What we can see, and what we can't

Everything on this page was built from publicly visible data — no login, no partnership, no pixel, nothing you had to give us. That is the proof of concept and also the limit. Here is precisely what is missing.

Available from outside

  • Likes and comment counts, every post, every market
  • Reel play counts — a genuine reach denominator for 63% of output
  • Format, timestamp to the minute, pinned status
  • Follower counts per market account
  • Caption text and hashtags

Invisible without access

  • Reach on carousels and stills — 37% of output has no denominator at all
  • Saves and shares — the signals that actually predict intent
  • Follower vs. non-follower split — reaching new climbers, or the same regulars?
  • Profile taps and link clicks — the bridge from post to day pass
  • Paid amplification — a boosted post silently corrupts any organic baseline
  • Membership outcomes — whether any of this fills the gym
Under the hood

Method

Why the statistics are built this way
  1. Engagement rate, not raw counts. (likes + comments) / followers. Without it, Seattle at 27,154 followers beats Boston at 6,711 by construction and the comparison measures audience size, not content.
  2. Log space. Social engagement is log-normal with a long right tail. One breakout reel pulls a raw mean above most of the data.
  3. Median and MAD (×1.4826), never mean and standard deviation. Using the mean to find outliers is circular — the outliers move the mean.
  4. Baselines are per-market and per-format. A carousel in Tempe is not comparable to a reel in Seattle, and pretending otherwise is the most common way this analysis goes wrong.
  5. Leave-one-out scoring. Each post is measured against its peers excluding itself, so a post never influences the bar it is judged against.
  6. Robust z is comparable across markets by construction. Each post is measured in its own market's units, which is what makes the network rollups legitimate rather than a disguised follower-count ranking.
  7. Pinned posts excluded. They sit at the top of a profile accumulating engagement indefinitely; eleven were removed before any statistic was computed.
  8. An explicit "no baseline" state. Under eight comparable posts, the system returns nothing. Refusing to answer is a feature — a system that always has an opinion is guessing.
  9. Confounds not controlled for. Posting time of day, day of week, whether a post was boosted, whether a climber with reach re-shared it, seasonality, and a three-fold spread in posting cadence between markets. Every one is fixable with account access; none are fixable from outside.

Post-level aggregates only. No commenter identities, profile photos, or tagged-user records were retained at any point in the pipeline.
